To start with successes, I was able to mount it, I calibrated it up to about 650 lbs, and I got a somewhat reliable readout upon testing weights after the calibration. The first challenge, was mounting my sensor. I planned to use magnets to hold it up, but I used magents to attatch my circuit board to a metal plate and then I stuck the metal plate to my car. I then spent a significant amount of time trying to figure out why it was wokring, then not working then working again seemingly random. But, it turns out that the metal was stopping it from working. My guess is that some wires may have been poking through the board, hitting the metal and shorting. I ended up solving this issue by taping my sensor to a large plastic container and taping a magnet to that plastic and then attaching it. This worked great on my truck, but I then went to test it on a different truck and found a more difficult mounting area. The rear differential, where I mounted on my truck, was covered in houses and wires and random things that made it very hard to stick my sensor on. Eventually I got it attatched at a not perfect angle, but it worked. </p> <!-- IMAGE GRID 2 --> <div style="display:flex; flex-wrap:wrap; gap:10px; justify-content:center;"> <img src="oncar1.png" style="width:45%; height:auto;"> <img src="oncar2.png" style="width:45%; height:auto;"> </div> <p style="color:black; font-size:22px;"> The next problem was in the readout consistency. Upon my first test, the readout was changing by hundreds of pounds a second, while the actual weight was unchanging. I then increased the stability of the readout with a few changes. The first is by taking a slow exponential moving average, so instead of reading the exact current distance, it reads the average of the last few seconds. The other is a minimum change threshold which ignores minor changes in distance under a few mm. <br><br> The issue with this is I am dealing with huge amounts of weight, 0 - 1000+ lbs, but only small distance changes, less than 40 mm. Minor distance changes correspond to large weight changes, but the sensor naturaly has some minor variation in its distance reading. Even steady on a table, it will vary within a few mm which can correspond to a couple hundred pounds on a car. This variation is worsened when the sensor is not at a perfect 90 degree angle, which as I described early was very difficult to acheive. <br><br> I ended up doing a few different tests, gradually increasing or decreasing the stability of the readout by reducing responsiveness and gaining more consistent readout, or by increasing responsiveness and increasing how much the readout jumps around. The final version worked OK. It game me a relatively consistent readout, that would occasionally jump by 150 lbs or so, and was somewhat accurate. For example, when testing, 150 lbs might read 225 lbs instead or zero lbs was reading between 35 and 65. Exactly why this is I don't know. My guess is that because the sensor varies, when I mark my calibration point, it is possible I mark it at the high or low end of the average instantaneously, and then when reading the weight, I am taking the average so these numbers dont match up. I think in my next version, I am going to try to take the calibration point as an average. I would add weight, start calibration and then wait 5 or so seconds and it would then mark the average distance as that weight. This wont the stability of the readout, but it should improve accuracy slightly. <br><br> Below is my full code that I would upload for use on a truck. <br><br> <p class="margin"> </p> <div class="flexrow"> <a id="btn" href="calibrationv9.zip" download> View Code </a> </div> <p class="margin"> </p> <p style="color:black; font-size:22px;"> For the in class demo, I built a small contraption to model what happens when you weight the bed of a truck. The model features two springs supporting a flat top above the sensor, which slides in and out through the side. As you add weight to the top, the springs compress, decreasing the distance between the sensor and the top. I mounted the springs to the base and top using short pieces of metal pipe that sit in holes in the wood and slide into the springs. In order to keep the weights on top and and keep it level, I added walls to the model that are fixed to the base, but loose against the top, ensuring it stays centered as the springs compress.
